An Australian regulator has drawn a sharper line around what an AI scribe is allowed to do before it may become a medical device.

In a review published on **16 October 2026**, the Therapeutic Goods Administration said eight of 12 digital-scribe products it examined included functions **likely to meet the definition of a medical device**. Two needed more information and two appeared unlikely to be devices based on the information available. None of the 12 suppliers had identified its product as a medical device.

This is a late-discovery briefing about that six-day-old record. It is not evidence that the regulator tested the products, found them unsafe or reached a final published ruling about every named product.

### A function can change the regulatory boundary

TGA began with an environmental scan of roughly 100 suppliers operating in Australia. Forty responded to a voluntary survey, and the regulator focused on 12 products spanning large and smaller suppliers. It reviewed public information, documentation, labelling, marketing material and descriptions of functionality and intended purpose, as well as information submitted by clinicians and patients.

The distinction in the resulting guidance is functional. Software that only records, transcribes, translates, organises or summarises a consultation—without adding new clinical content—may remain an administrative tool. A function becomes more likely to be a medical device when it identifies or suggests diagnoses, recommends treatments or investigations, prioritises patients, monitors them, or generates patient-specific clinical conclusions.

That means a single product can contain both regulated and non-regulated functions. It also means a general disclaimer such as “not for diagnosis” does not settle the question when the product's design, marketing or actual features point to a therapeutic purpose.

Some clinical decision-support software can be exempt from inclusion in the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods. But exempt software may still be a regulated medical device and must satisfy the conditions of the exemption and other applicable requirements.

### What the review did not establish

The regulator was explicit about its limits. It did **not** test any product, check safety or performance, assess privacy or data protection, or perform a detailed compliance assessment. The 12-product group was selected after a broader scan and was not designed to estimate how common regulated functions are across the entire market.

The reported result is therefore a regulatory screening finding about functions and intended purpose. It is not a ranking of products, a clinical-utility study or proof of patient benefit or harm. The report does not publish product versions or final classifications for each named supplier.

TGA said it will use the review to guide education, further guidance and targeted, proportionate compliance activity. The public record does not identify a completed enforcement action arising from the review.

### The practical question for buyers

For healthcare organisations, “Is this an AI scribe?” is less useful than asking what each enabled function actually does. Procurement and governance teams need to map features and claims: Does the tool merely restructure words already supplied by a clinician, or does it select clinical information, infer significance, produce a conclusion or recommend what happens next?

That review should be repeated when a supplier adds features. TGA's guidance says a therapeutic function introduced in an update can change a product's status. In Australia, that may require the supplier to stop supplying the product until it is included in the register, remove the therapeutic function, or establish that the function qualifies for the clinical decision-support exemption.

These are Australian regulatory boundaries. They should not be treated as legal conclusions for the European Union, Luxembourg or another jurisdiction. But the operational lesson travels: assess the exact function, version, claims and intended use rather than relying on the product category or a disclaimer.
