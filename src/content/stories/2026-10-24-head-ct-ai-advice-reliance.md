Giving clinicians an explanation for an AI recommendation did not remove a basic human-factors problem: people can follow bad advice, but they can also reject good advice.

A randomized crossover experiment published in _The Lancet Digital Health_ on **23 October 2026** tested both behaviors during a simplified head CT task. Fifty radiologists and 75 non-radiologist physicians or medical trainees reviewed the same 50 cases under three conditions: no AI, a binary AI recommendation, or a recommendation paired with an explanatory bounding box.

The study does not evaluate a commercial system. The researchers generated the advice and deliberately made it correct on 80% of trials. That design lets them study reliance under controlled errors, but it cannot estimate what would happen with a deployed model whose accuracy, confidence and error patterns change across hospitals.

### Correct advice helped, but explanations were not a safeguard

When explainable advice was correct, diagnostic performance improved relative to the no-AI condition. The reported mixed-effects odds ratio was **1.84**, with a 95% confidence interval from **1.49 to 2.28**. Confidence also increased, while the final abstract did not find a statistically significant reduction in reading time.

Wrong advice still pulled decisions in the wrong direction. Depending on expertise and advice condition, participants followed incorrect recommendations on **27% to 58%** of eligible trials. The accepted manuscript also reports under-reliance—rejecting correct advice—on **11% to 21%** of eligible trials.

Because four of every five recommendations were correct by design, under-reliance affected more cases in absolute terms even though its rate was lower. That is an important arithmetic point, not a universal property of clinical AI. A system with lower accuracy, a different threshold or a shifted patient population could reverse the balance.

The explanation layer also had uneven effects. Bounding boxes could help participants use correct advice, but they did not neutralize bad recommendations. The authors report that explainable false-positive advice could reduce performance among novices on otherwise easy cases.

### The experiment isolates behavior by simplifying medicine

Every participant worked through no-AI, basic-advice and explainable-advice sessions, separated by at least 14 days. The analysis used participants and cases as random effects. The project was preregistered, and de-identified data, analysis code and mixed-methods materials are available through OSF.

Those strengths come with deliberate simplifications. The task was a binary judgment about intracranial hemorrhage. The 50-case set contained 25 positive and 25 negative cases, far from routine prevalence, and difficult cases were oversampled. One hundred eleven of 125 participants completed all three sessions; the analysis included everyone who started the first.

The “novice” group combined medical trainees and physicians outside radiology, while the expert group included radiologists with varied experience. The setting was a web experiment, not an emergency department or reporting workstation. No treatment decisions, delays, downstream tests, patient outcomes or actual safety events were measured.

### Measure both directions of reliance

Oversight plans often focus on automation bias: will a user accept an incorrect model output? This study shows why the mirror error belongs in the same evaluation. If clinicians routinely dismiss correct recommendations, a tool can fail to deliver expected benefit even without causing spectacular false positives.

That does not mean users should trust the system more. Appropriate reliance depends on calibration: the advice should be accepted when it is likely to be right and challenged when it is likely to be wrong. The experiment's fixed 80% accuracy created one such environment; a hospital needs evidence from its own model, prevalence, interface and users.

For implementation teams, the durable requirement is to test both failure modes. Explanation interfaces should be evaluated under deliberately wrong as well as correct advice, across easy and difficult cases, with subgroup, confidence and workflow measures. A visually persuasive explanation is not itself evidence that the recommendation deserves trust.
