# Science-writing standard for HealthTech AI Pulse

**Adopted 21 October 2026.** This is the practical companion to [EDITORIAL.md](../EDITORIAL.md), which remains authoritative on evidence and medical claims. Aspire to the accessibility, curiosity and rigorous reporting associated with excellent science magazines, without imitating another publication's prose, inventing reporting, or manufacturing drama.

## Reader-first reporting, not a research abstract

Write for an intelligent, curious reader who knows healthcare matters but may not know a clinical abbreviation, study design or statistical convention. The opening should answer **why this specific development matters** in one or two plain sentences, backed by the verified record. Reveal the real tension: a model can score well yet fail at a human handoff; a regulator can describe a safeguard without approving a device; a promising study can be limited to one site. A scene must be observed and sourced, not invented.

Keep an **evidence spine** throughout the story: _what happened → what researchers or authorities actually did → what they found or said → what the evidence cannot tell us → what deserves attention next_. This is a narrative progression, **not five compulsory subheadings**. Choose an order appropriate to the event. A short announcement may need only four paragraphs; a complex study may need two useful subheadings.

When covering a study, put the design and sample next to the finding they qualify. Distinguish patients from images, readings, encounters and sites. Explain absolute numbers, units, denominators and uncertainty in reader language. A result from 14 clinicians is not a result from 1,750 independent people. Expand clinical terms at first use, where a five-word gloss can prevent an entire paragraph of confusion.

Write the first draft from the claim ledger and inspected primary evidence, **not by paraphrasing the press release or copying the source abstract paragraph by paragraph**. Source quotations must be authentic and attributed; do not invent conversations, patients, lab scenes, motives or sensory detail.

## The voice

- **Specific rather than promotional.** Name the technology, study group, location, date and consequence when they matter. Prefer “In a simulation involving 14 clinicians…” over “A groundbreaking study reveals…”.
- **Human-readable rather than simplified into inaccuracy.** Explain “pneumoperitoneum” as air in the abdominal cavity; preserve the clinical term only when useful. Explain “predetermined change control plan” before using “PCCP”.
- **Concrete rather than padded.** Each paragraph should add a new verified fact, explanatory context or reasoned consequence. Avoid repetitions of “this does not establish” in three adjacent sections.
- **Curious, not breathless.** A surprising difference is interesting; a subgroup of three people cannot justify a sweeping profession-level claim.
- **Independent rather than corporate.** Describe the sponsor's objective as an objective, not a demonstrated benefit.
- **Confident about facts, frank about gaps.** Prefer “The study did not measure patient outcomes” to vague “More research is needed.”

Avoid recurrent machine-written markers: “In a rapidly evolving landscape”, “game changer”, “delve into”, “it is worth noting”, “the implications are profound”, “a pivotal moment”, rigid “What this does—and does not—show” titles for every story, and an obligatory “Editorial interpretation:” paragraph. Clearly distinguish analysis from reporting, but the distinction can be conveyed in natural prose (“For hospitals considering this system, the next test would be…”), or labeled when a recommendation might otherwise be mistaken for a study finding.

## Fit the structure to the evidence

| Story type                         | Lead with                                    | Then make clear                                                                         |
| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Original clinical/biomedical study | The question and bounded finding             | Design, sample, setting, meaningful numbers, uncertainty, clinical boundary             |
| Device regulation or policy        | The actual action, jurisdiction and date     | What changed in practice, what was _not_ authorised or required                         |
| Corporate partnership/tool launch  | What exists today, not the pitch             | Intended deployment versus demonstrated use, independence and next verifiable milestone |
| Safety or contradiction            | The verified failure mode or disputed result | Source of signal, exposure/denominators, competing explanations and practical checks    |

## Editorial acceptance checklist

A reviewer should be able to answer yes to each of the following:

1. Would the first two sentences matter to a reader who has not yet seen the study or press release?
2. Can a non-specialist explain the result and _who/what was measured_ after one read?
3. Is each headline, number, comparison, date and quote traceable to a primary source or properly attributed secondary reporting?
4. Are the main qualification and access limitations close to the claims they constrain rather than hidden in a boilerplate ending?
5. Does the article progress without repetitive stock headings, disclaimers, abstract-style phrasing or padded bullets?
6. Does the practical consequence follow from the evidence without drifting into medical advice, certainty or unsupported causation?
7. Does the mandatory photographic concept illuminate this exact story without pretending to document actual participants, outcomes or a named institution?

If an answer is no, revise the draft or withhold the story. Automated spelling/readability metrics are aids, not permission to skip editorial review. A human editor must inspect the live reading layout and image together.

## Example: moving from abstract to story

**Mechanical:** “A prospective single-group usability evaluation demonstrated reported workload reductions with limitations in generalisability.”

**Reader-first:** “In one oncology department, clinicians reported less work preparing consultations with an AI assistant. But a more revealing problem appeared when the summary reached the hospital record: missing allergy information could become an incorrect reassurance.”

Both sentences still need the underlying primary-source check. The example does **not** add an interview, a patient outcome, a causal claim or a generalised benefit.
